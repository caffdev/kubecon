One of the first things worth deciding from the lake is how big every pod should be. This is lakeside savings, in practice. One workload, one trading week. The white line is what it actually uses: busy in market hours, a spike at the open and the close, quiet overnight, nearly idle at the weekend. The dashed blue line is what VPA asks for. VPA's recommender looks at history and settles on one size with headroom over the peaks, and that's a good, safe floor. But it's one number for the whole week. The teal line is what an AI can ask for when it reads the lake: the market calendar, the deploy history, the Kafka lag, last week's same day. Tight overnight and at the weekend, raised before the open, back up for the close. The shaded gap between the two lines is reserved capacity we stop paying for. On this made-up workload it's a large share of the core-hours. How it works without anything restarting: VPA still sets the floor; in-place resize changes the size of a running pod; KEDA reacts to fast signals like queue depth; and Karpenter consolidates the nodes that frees up. That last step is where the bill actually goes down. And the guardrail, because this is an agent changing production. It starts by proposing the new sizes as a pull request. Only later does it act on its own, inside minimum and maximum bounds that policy enforces, with automatic rollback. Hertz showed this pattern at the show: AI agents right-sizing every workload and rearranging nodes across their EKS fleet, about eighty clusters, rolled out through Terraform. They didn't post slides, so the sixty-one percent here is our illustration of the pattern, not their number. And the "Agentic Karpenter" demo had Claude propose fixes for a human to approve. And that's the pattern for every agent on this map: read from the lake, propose first, act only inside bounds that policy enforces. Which brings back the third way agents arrive, the one we can't fence out.
