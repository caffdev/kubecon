Multi-cluster has a second problem that looks like fleet toil and isn't: losing a region. Confusing the two is how you end up buying a scheduler for a traffic problem. "No user-visible downtime" isn't a number. It's a sum of delays, and customers see the slowest one. Placement is solved. ApplicationSets already run the same app in both regions. Click. And that's the only lane a multi-cluster scheduler like Karmada shortens. Karmada is good, Bloomberg runs it in production, but it stays on our watch list until we can name a workload that needs scheduling rather than placement. That's decision 2: which production workloads can't run active-active? If that list is empty, the decision closes. The long lanes are somewhere else. Traffic: something has to notice the region is gone, and the change has to propagate. Then clients keep the old DNS answer cached as long as they like, which lands inside our recovery time and out of our control. That's why we recommend AWS Global Accelerator at the edge: it takes the DNS cache off the clock. Our recommendation, to be clear, not something we saw presented. State: we can't fail traffic over faster than the data. Aurora Global or DynamoDB global tables have to converge in the survivor, and doing it in the other order is how writes split. Capacity: the survivor inherits a hundred percent of traffic at half its size. Either we pay for permanent double headroom and this lane goes to zero, or Karpenter's scale-up time is formally part of our RTO. The lengths are illustrative; measuring them is decision 2b.
