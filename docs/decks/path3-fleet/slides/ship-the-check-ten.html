The second design rule is how you know a bulk change actually landed. It has nothing to do with AI, and it's the best template we saw all week for anything we'll later want a machine to drive. Ship every change together with the check that fails without it. NVIDIA had two kernel CVEs to fix in one week, across clouds and on-prem. The fix shipped with its own check, the check fails until the fix has really landed on that node, and every node reports a status. So the picture on the left is the audit. Every square is a node, about fifty-six hundred of them, converged in thirty minutes across sixteen clusters. Most are verified, a few are still pending, and one failed and is being retried. The status mix is illustrative; the pattern and the numbers are theirs. And on the right is how they read it: one kubectl get on their rollout object. Status in progress, forty-eight eighty of five thousand nodes complete. Done is an enum value on the resource, and the nodes that need attention are the failed Jobs. Where the fleet stands is a query, not a spreadsheet someone fills in. Their own line for what came next: the next CVE is a YAML change. That's fleet toil. Losing a region looks like the same problem, and it isn't.
