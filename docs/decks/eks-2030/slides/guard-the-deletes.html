Nutanix, “GitOps Meltdown” (posted slides; they ran Flux, the shape is the same in Argo CD). “Automation is only as safe as the desired state it trusts.”

This was the cautionary tale of the show. Nutanix ran thirty-nine production clusters from one management cluster through GitOps. An admin, changing something else, deleted the top-level object of the tree. Pruning did exactly what it was built to do: it deleted everything under it. The timeline: twelve minutes in, the delete stalled, because the GitOps controller had deleted itself. Eight hours later they restored the controller, it read the same desired state, and finished the job: all thirty-nine clusters gone. Two days to get the clusters back, two more for the apps. And the backups were twelve hours old and had never been restore-tested. They used Flux. The same shape exists in Argo CD's app-of-apps. Four guards, all basic. Click. One: no single root owns the fleet. One Application per cluster, and Argo CD bootstrapped on its own. Click. Two: make bulk delete hard. The Prune=false sync option on clusters and stateful infrastructure, and an in-tree CEL admission policy that refuses deletes on them. We'll check the exact option names against our Argo CD version. Click. Three: data outlives objects. Retain on the storage class, and Git hosted outside the clusters it drives. Click. Four: restore on a schedule. Their new targets are an hour of data loss and thirty minutes to restore, proven on a game day. Move two is about the apps, and keeping Argo CD boring.
