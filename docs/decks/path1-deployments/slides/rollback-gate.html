If you remember one mechanism from this deck, make it this one, because rollback is the control people trust a machine with least. A Rollout is the workload's spec, in Git. It sends a share of traffic to the new version and pauses. An AnalysisRun queries a metric, error rate or latency, and judges it against a threshold we wrote down in advance. Click. When the analysis fails, the version goes back to the last good one. Nobody is paged to make the call and nobody has to notice a graph. A canary that waits for a human to look at a dashboard is just a slower outage. The bars underneath are why. They're LinkedIn's, drawn to the same scale. A loud webhook outage was caught in about thirty minutes. A silent webhook change that stripped GPUs from jobs ran for about two days, eight hundred and twenty executions. Nobody watching a graph catches the quiet ones. A gate does. Two honest notes, if asked. A weighted canary needs a traffic router, and which one is the networking thread's call; the Rollouts Gateway API plugin is unverified, so we check its docs first. And for platform services with no traffic to split, LinkedIn's answer needs no mesh: a canary in its own namespace, one label as the switch, and an Argo CD PostSync Job as the gate. That's why canary is a proof of concept per workload class, and the gate is the part that isn't optional. That's rollback. The other two controls turn out to be one policy set.
